Sensible

Real-time awareness of your environment so you can stay calm and in control.

Date March 2026
Role Solo Designer
Tools Figma
Duration 4 Days
The Problem

Humans experience sensory overload when sound, light and environmental stimuli exceed what the brain can comfortably process. Millions of people feel overwhelmed by noise, light, crowds and constant stimulation, and offices, schools and public spaces keep adding to that input.

The hardest part is noticing it in time. People usually realize they're overwhelmed only once stress or anxiety has already started, and there is no way to measure it. Heart rate and sleep have clear metrics, but sensory stimulation doesn't.

The Opportunity

What if we could measure sensory environments the same way we measure heart rate?

That question became the core of the project: a new measurable signal called the Sensory Overload Score. It is a single, simple number that describes how much sensory load an environment is putting on you, and it alerts you before you reach your threshold.

The Solution

A real-time sensory scoring system that helps users anticipate overload.

Sensible monitors the environment and the body continuously. It looks at light, sound and crowd density around you, along with your heart rate, and combines them into one score. When the score starts to climb, the app warns you early and suggests what to do, like moving somewhere quieter or opening Calm Mode.

Design Process

From idea to prototype in four days.

FigBuild gave me four days to take Sensible from a question to a working prototype. I worked alone, so every decision from the first sketch to the final video was mine. Here is how those days went.

Day 1 · March 6Ideation and wireframes

I started by planning the idea. The goal was to make sensory load something a person could glance at, so the app needed one clear number, and a small set of inputs behind it: light, sound, crowd density and heart rate. With that settled, I began wireframing the screens the idea would need.

Day 2 · March 7High-fidelity prototype

I finished the wireframes and moved on to a high-fidelity prototype. The first screen people meet is the welcome screen, so I used it to explain the app in three short ideas: understand your sensory environment, prevent overload with early alerts, and regulate and recover with tools like Calm Mode.

Each idea has its own small icon and a short description, and a single blue Continue button leads into the app. For someone who may already be overstimulated, I wanted the first screen to be calm and quick to read.

Day 3 · March 8Key flows

With the prototype finished, I connected the key flows that guide someone through the app. The home screen is the centre of it. The largest panel shows the current Sensory Overload Score as a percentage with a plain-language label and a small trend line, so you can see both where you are and where you've been heading.

Below it, four smaller readings explain what's behind the score: heart rate, sound level, stress index and crowd density. Each one has a short word such as "Comfortable" or "Moderate" so nobody has to interpret raw numbers. Each reading has its own soft pastel colour, which makes the screen easy to scan without being harsh. Bottom navigation links Home, History and Calendar, and the Calm Mode button sits in the middle of the readings, always within reach.

The most important flow is what happens when the score rises. Tapping the Calm Mode button replaces the whole interface with a dark navy screen, a softly glowing circle and one short instruction to breathe along with it. Everything else is removed on purpose. At the moment someone needs help most, the app should add as little stimulation as possible.

Day 4 · March 9Presentation and submission

On the last day I put together the presentation and the video to submit to FigBuild 2026.

Use Cases

Who benefits from Sensible?

A student in a lecture hall

A student enters a crowded lecture hall where overlapping conversations, bright lighting and movement quickly raise sensory stimulation. Sensible detects the rising load and alerts the student before it becomes overwhelming. It recommends moving to a quieter seat or activating Calm Mode to reduce distractions.

A commuter on the subway

During a busy commute, noise spikes, crowd density and motion all add to sensory stress. Sensible keeps monitoring these inputs and predicts when overload may occur. The commuter gets a warning and suggestions like stepping off for a break or turning on noise reduction.

An office worker

In an open office, conversations, screens and movement make stimulation rise and fall throughout the day. Sensible picks out patterns and highlights the peak periods of sensory stress, so the employee can schedule breaks or move to a quieter workspace to keep their focus.

Visual Design

A calm, low-stimulation palette.

This colour palette was designed to create a calm, low-stimulation visual environment that supports users who may already be experiencing sensory stress. Light blues and white keep most screens quiet, while the deep navy in Calm Mode removes as much visual noise as possible.

The Future

Sensory tracking as the next frontier of wellness.

As technology becomes part of daily life, tools that understand and support human sensory experiences will matter more. Smart buildings, classrooms and workplaces could adjust sensory stimuli automatically based on the person in them, and wearables could help people understand how their bodies react to different environments.

Just as fitness trackers helped us monitor physical health, sensory tracking may become an essential tool for mental and emotional balance. Sensible is my first step toward that idea.
